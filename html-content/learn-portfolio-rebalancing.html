Portfolio Rebalancing: Keep Asset Allocation on Track

You started with a clean 60% equity and 40% debt allocation. One bull market later, equity has grown to 78% of your portfolio. You're now taking far more risk than you intended — and you don't even know it. Portfolio rebalancing is the discipline of periodically bringing your allocation back in line with your risk tolerance and financial goals.

What is Portfolio Rebalancing and Why Does Allocation Drift?

Portfolio rebalancing is the process of selling overperforming assets and buying underperforming ones to restore your target asset allocation. It sounds counterintuitive — you're selling what's winning and buying what's lagging. But this discipline forces you to systematically sell high and buy low, which is the fundamental rule of investing that most people get exactly backwards.

Allocation drift happens naturally. If equity markets rise 30% in a year while debt returns 7%, a starting 60/40 portfolio shifts to roughly 67/33 without you doing anything. After a 3-year bull market, that 60/40 could become 80/20. Suddenly you're carrying the risk of an aggressive investor's portfolio even though you planned for a balanced one.

The 2021 example: An investor who started 2020 with a 60/40 equity-debt portfolio and never rebalanced saw their equity allocation swell to 76% by the end of 2021 after the massive post-COVID rally. They took far more risk heading into the 2022 correction and suffered proportionally larger losses.

Annual Rebalancing vs Threshold-Based Rebalancing

There are two common approaches to rebalancing:

Annual Rebalancing: Once a year (typically in April, the start of the Indian financial year), check your allocation and bring it back to target. Simple, predictable, low effort. This is the approach most retail investors should use.

Threshold-Based Rebalancing: Rebalance whenever any asset class drifts more than a set percentage (typically 20%) from its target. So if your equity target is 60%, you rebalance when it exceeds 72% or falls below 48%. This is more responsive but requires more active monitoring.

How to Rebalance Without Triggering Unnecessary Tax

The biggest concern with rebalancing is capital gains tax. Selling equity that's appreciated triggers LTCG or STCG. However, there's a smarter approach that avoids selling (and thus avoids tax) in most cases:

Redirect new SIP flows: Instead of selling equity, simply direct all new investments into debt until the allocation rebalances naturally. If equity is at 72% and you want 60%, stop equity SIPs temporarily and channel all new money into a debt fund. This rebalances over 3–6 months with zero capital gains tax.

Only sell existing equity units if the drift is extreme (30%+) and you need to rebalance quickly — for example, as you approach a financial goal like retirement or a home purchase and need to reduce risk urgently.

Tools to Monitor Your Allocation

Check your allocation quarterly at minimum, and once per year take deliberate action to rebalance if needed.

Rebalancing as You Near Your Goal

The most critical time to rebalance is 3–5 years before you need the money. A 30-year-old saving for retirement can afford to be 80% equity. But a 55-year-old planning to retire at 60 should gradually shift to 40–50% equity and 50–60% debt. This "glide path" approach protects you from a market crash wiping out years of gains just before you need to draw down. Rebalancing in the final years is not optional — it's essential risk management.
