Expense Ratio: The Silent Killer of Long-Term Wealth

S
Siddharth Roy
•7 min read•January 2026
Listen to Article

Nobody talks about expense ratios when they're excitedly sharing their mutual fund returns. But over 30 years, a 1% annual fee quietly steals 26% of your total wealth. This isn't a trivial number — on a ₹3 crore corpus it's ₹78 lakh gone. Here's how to spot it, understand it, and fight it.

What is an Expense Ratio?

An expense ratio is the annual fee that a mutual fund charges to manage your money. It covers fund manager salaries, administration costs, marketing, and compliance. It's expressed as a percentage of your total assets in the fund and is deducted daily from the fund's NAV — meaning you never see a bill. It's already priced into the returns you see on your screen.

For example, if a fund has an NAV of ₹100 and an expense ratio of 1.5%, the fund deducts approximately ₹1.50 per unit per year to cover its operating costs. This happens automatically, 365 days a year, whether markets go up or down. Even in a year where you lose 10%, the expense ratio is still deducted.

Direct Plans vs Regular Plans: The Hidden 0.5–1% Gap

Every mutual fund in India has two variants: a Regular Plan and a Direct Plan. Regular plans are sold through distributors (brokers, banks), who earn a commission of 0.5–1.5% per year. This commission is embedded in the expense ratio of the regular plan. Direct plans cut out the middleman — you invest directly with the AMC, and the expense ratio is significantly lower.

The difference seems small: a regular plan might have an expense ratio of 1.8%, while the direct plan of the same fund is 1.1%. That's a gap of 0.7%. But compounded over 30 years, that 0.7% difference on a ₹10 lakh corpus growing at 12% becomes the difference between ₹2.2 crore (regular) and ₹3.0 crore (direct) — a gap of ₹80 lakh. You're essentially paying ₹80 lakh to a distributor over your lifetime for doing nothing.

The 30-Year Compounding Calculation

Let's do the exact math. Two investors put ₹10,000/month in identical equity funds for 30 years, both earning 12% gross returns. The only difference: Investor A is in a direct plan (total expense 0.8%) and Investor B is in a regular plan (total expense 1.5%):

  • Investor A (Direct, 11.2% net return): ₹3.8 crore at the end
  • Investor B (Regular, 10.5% net return): ₹3.0 crore at the end
  • Difference: ₹80 lakh — pure compounding loss to fees

The total amount invested by both was ₹36 lakh (₹10,000 × 360 months). Investor B paid an extra ₹80 lakh in fees — which is 2.2x their total investment. That's how brutal long-term fee drag is.

SEBI's Expense Ratio Caps

SEBI mandates maximum expense ratios for Indian mutual funds, and they decrease as the fund's assets grow larger. For equity funds, the maximum is 2.25% for funds with AUM up to ₹500 crore and drops to 1.05% for very large funds. For debt funds, caps are lower. Index funds and ETFs have no active management, so their expense ratios are typically 0.05–0.20%. SEBI requires all funds to disclose their TER (Total Expense Ratio) daily on their website.

How to Find the Lowest Expense Ratio

On every fund page in MF Bharat, you'll see the current expense ratio prominently displayed. For equity index funds, look for anything under 0.15%. For actively managed equity funds, a well-managed fund in direct plan should be under 1.0%. Anything above 1.5% in an actively managed equity fund warrants serious scrutiny — the fund manager needs to consistently outperform the benchmark by at least that margin just to break even for you.
